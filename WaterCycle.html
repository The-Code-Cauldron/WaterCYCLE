The Four Forms

Which shape are you?

Based on the VARK model (Neil Fleming, 1987). Four distinct cognitive styles. Most people have one dominant form and a secondary. Very few are purely one thing.

Form I
Visual
The Seer
You think in pictures. Ideas arrive as images, diagrams, spatial relationships. When someone explains something, you are simultaneously building a mental map of it. When you close your eyes to remember something, you see it.
Natural tools
Mind mapsDiagramsCharts Colour codingVideosInfographics FlowchartsSpatial notes
Study technique
Convert everything into a visual. Redraw your notes as diagrams. Use colour to encode categories. Sketch the relationship between ideas before writing about them. Watch before reading.
What slows you down
Walls of unbroken text. Lectures with no slides or visual aids. Being asked to absorb abstract verbal descriptions with nothing to look at. Dense paragraphs that describe something a diagram would show in two seconds.
You recognise yourself in this
You remember faces better than names. You instinctively grab a pen to draw when explaining something. You prefer maps over written directions. You use colour in your notes. You find YouTube tutorials more useful than written guides. You doodle during lectures — not to be rude, but because your hands need to draw what your ears are hearing.
The Visual Protocol Before studying: sketch a mind map of what you already know. During: convert notes to diagrams immediately. After: draw the full picture from memory. Test yourself visually — cover diagrams and recreate them.
Best tools
Miro, Notion (with diagrams), Whimsical, pen and paper, YouTube, Anki (image-based), colour highlighters, sketchbooks for notes.
Known Visual thinkers
Albert Einstein Leonardo da Vinci Walt Disney Temple Grandin Steve Jobs
Form II
Auditory
The Listener
You think in sound and rhythm. Language spoken aloud is your medium. You process ideas by talking them through — with others or with yourself. You remember what was said far more easily than what was written.
Natural tools
PodcastsLecturesDiscussion AudiobooksVoice memosTeaching aloud Music as study aidStudy groups
Study technique
Read your notes out loud. Record yourself explaining concepts and play them back. Find a study partner to discuss ideas with. The Feynman technique is made for you — explain it aloud to an imaginary beginner. If you cannot explain it simply out loud, you have not absorbed it.
What slows you down
Silent study environments. Being expected to absorb only from text. Formats with no human voice involved. Instruction that says "read this" with no discussion opportunity. You switch off in silence.
You recognise yourself in this
You remember conversations better than documents. You talk to yourself when solving problems. You prefer to be told instructions rather than read them. You absorb podcasts on long drives without trying. You hum or create rhythms to remember things. You find music helps you concentrate rather than distracting you. You learn best from great teachers, not great textbooks.
The Auditory Protocol Record yourself summarising each topic after studying. Listen back on commutes. Find a podcast, lecture series, or audiobook on every subject you need to learn. Discuss everything — even with yourself.
Best tools
Audible, Spotify, Voice Memos, Otter.ai, YouTube lectures, study groups, language learning apps with audio (Pimsleur), text-to-speech on articles.
Known Auditory thinkers
Winston Churchill Barack Obama Oprah Winfrey Maya Angelou
Form III
Reading/Writing
The Scribe
You think in written language. Text is your natural medium. You process ideas by reading them carefully and writing about them. The act of writing is the act of thinking. Notes are not a record — they are how you understand.
Natural tools
BooksDetailed notesSummaries ListsDefinitionsResearch papers JournalingWritten essays
Study technique
Rewrite your notes in your own words. Summarise every chapter or concept in writing before moving on. Make glossaries and definitions. Write practice essays. The act of putting it in your own written words IS the learning — not a record of learning that already happened.
What slows you down
Purely verbal instruction with no text backup. Visual-only explanations with no written labels or notes. Being told to just go and do it without written guidance. Instructors who speak quickly and do not provide notes.
You recognise yourself in this
You take extensive notes — even when you do not need to. You instinctively write lists. You prefer written instructions. You annotate books. You process emotions by journaling. You prefer email over phone calls. You find that writing something down helps you remember it, even if you never read the note again. You trust what you read more than what you hear.
The Scribe Protocol Read actively — annotate everything. After each section, stop and write a summary in your own words without looking back. Before any test or presentation, write out everything you know from scratch. The blank page is your friend.
Best tools
Notion, Obsidian, physical notebooks, Anki (text-based), long-form reading (books, Substack, papers), journaling, writing apps, margin-annotation tools.
Known Reading/Writing thinkers
Charles Darwin Virginia Woolf Warren Buffett Bill Gates
Form IV
Kinesthetic
The Doer
You think through action and physical experience. Abstract ideas only make sense when connected to doing something concrete. Theory without practice is noise. You learn by making mistakes, adjusting, and doing it again.
Natural tools
Hands-on practiceReal examplesCase studies Building / makingRole-playField work Trial and errorPrototyping
Study technique
Build it. Make it. Act it out. Connect every abstract concept to a real-world example or physical action before moving on. Use case studies. Simulate the environment. The fastest path from theory to retention is always through your hands.
What slows you down
Being told without being allowed to try. Long passive learning sessions — reading, sitting, listening — without any doing. Systems that front-load theory before any application. Being marked down for trying before you were told to.
You recognise yourself in this
You assemble furniture without reading the instructions. You learn new software by opening it and clicking. You cannot sit still for long periods. You learn to drive by driving, not by reading about driving. You remember things you did far more clearly than things you read or heard. You need to connect theory to real life before it means anything to you. You get frustrated when you cannot yet practice.
The Doer Protocol Find the practical version of everything you need to learn first. Start doing before you feel ready. Use deliberate practice — identify the specific skill, do it repeatedly with feedback. Embrace mistakes. They are not failures. They are how you update.
Best tools
Hands-on courses (Coursera Labs, Codecademy, cooking classes), physical sport and craft, project-based learning, maker spaces, simulations, internships, apprenticeships.
Known Kinesthetic thinkers
Richard Branson Thomas Edison Nikola Tesla Michael Jordan

The God Principle

The breath is the spirit.

Every civilisation reached the same conclusion independently. The word for breath and the word for spirit are the same word. That is not coincidence. It is convergence on a single truth.

Pneuma
Greek — breath / spirit / soul
For the Greeks, pneuma was both the air drawn into the lungs and the animating force that separated the living from the dead. Stoic philosophy held that pneuma was the divine fire running through all matter. To breathe consciously was to participate in the breath of the universe itself.
Prana
Sanskrit — life force / breath
Pranayama — breath control — is the fourth limb of Patanjali's eight-limbed yoga, positioned between the physical postures and the inner practices of concentration and meditation. The Vedic texts held that mastery of prana was mastery of the mind. The breath is the handle on the otherwise uncontrollable current of thought.
Ruah
Hebrew — breath / spirit / wind
Genesis 2:7 — "He breathed into his nostrils the breath of life, and the man became a living being." Ruah is the same word for wind, breath, and spirit. Consciousness itself delivered by breath. To breathe fully is to receive what was given. To breathe shallowly is to refuse it.
The Physiology
The diaphragm is threaded with vagus nerve fibres. Slow, deep diaphragmatic breathing activates those fibres directly — triggering a parasympathetic shift that drops cortisol, raises serotonin, and opens the brain's capacity for retention and integration. The pineal gland — long associated with the seat of consciousness and called the third eye across traditions — is most active in states of deep stillness. Shallow chest breathing keeps the nervous system in sympathetic dominance: the threat state. You cannot learn well under threat. The cocoon is where you exit threat and enter receive. A caterpillar's cocoon is total stillness before total transformation. What enters the cocoon and what emerges are not the same thing. Two minutes of controlled breath changes your neurochemistry. The traditions discovered this. The science confirmed it. The deeper you breathe, the better the connectivity.
